Our first major project

Grow a tree. Solve an engineering problem. Change a neighborhood.

Our first flagship project combines urban forestry, water conservation, engineering, environmental science, and a student competition. The goal is not simply to plant trees. It is to develop a repeatable system that helps trees survive in difficult urban conditions.

The Passive Tree Irrigation Challenge

How long can a tree survive with one carefully designed water supply?

Students design a low-cost irrigation system that delivers water to a newly planted tree over the longest practical period. The system should work passively, without electricity, electronic sensors, or routine manual operation.

At the beginning, a truck or other approved water source can fill the storage tanks. After filling, the system should deliver water gradually and reliably. Students are encouraged to experiment with gravity-fed drip systems, buried clay pots (ollas), wicking systems, subsurface delivery, slow-release reservoirs, and hybrid designs.

PHASE 01

Research & design

Teams study tree water needs, Houston soil and weather, passive irrigation principles, container reuse, and safety. They submit drawings, a materials list, a cost estimate, and an operating explanation.

PHASE 02

Prototype competition

Teams build and test systems under comparable conditions. Measure water delivery, leakage, clogging, evaporation, durability, and how long the system can provide useful irrigation.

PHASE 03

Real-world planting

Selected designs are adapted for approved Houston sites. Students and mentors install systems, document initial conditions, and monitor tree health and system performance over time.

Where will we plant?

We want to prioritize places that need shade—not simply the easiest places to plant. Potential sites include hot pedestrian corridors, exposed public spaces, school or community properties, and neighborhoods with limited tree canopy. Every site requires landowner or agency permission, utility checks, a suitable species, and a realistic maintenance plan.

What kind of trees?

Students can investigate native and well-adapted species, including shade trees and suitable fruit-bearing trees. Species selection should consider Houston's heat, flooding, drought periods, soil, mature size, roots, maintenance, and whether fruit can be safely and responsibly shared.

How will we measure impact?

Record tree species, planting date, site conditions, canopy and shade, water supplied, refill frequency, system failures, survival, growth, maintenance hours, and costs. Where practical, compare shaded and unshaded surface temperatures and document changes as the tree grows.

What happens to the winning designs?

We aim to publish build instructions, cost breakdowns, test results, and lessons learned so schools, community groups, and other neighborhoods can reproduce and improve the best designs.
